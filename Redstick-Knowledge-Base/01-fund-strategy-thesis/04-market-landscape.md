# Market Landscape

The macro case for the thesis, why now, and the competitive map. Reviewed **quarterly**
(see [`../../operating-cadence.md`](../../operating-cadence.md)); treat specific figures
below as directional and refresh them from primary sources at each review.

## Why food productivity, why now

Three forces are converging:

1. **Structural labor shortage.** Food production, processing, and service are among
   the hardest sectors to staff. Aging workforces, tightening immigration, and
   physically punishing work make labor scarce and expensive. This is the demand-side
   engine for the **Labour Productivity (Robotics)** vector.
2. **Technology finally deployable.** Robotics hardware costs have fallen while
   capability has risen; computer vision and production-grade AI can now run on the
   edge, in a plant, at speed. Problems that were research projects five years ago are
   now product.
3. **Margin and resilience pressure.** Thin food margins, input-cost volatility,
   perishability, and supply-chain fragility (exposed repeatedly this decade) make any
   durable productivity gain immediately valuable to a food business's P&L.

Meanwhile capital is disproportionately chasing horizontal AI. Food-productivity
technology is **under-capitalized relative to its TAM** — favorable entry dynamics for
a disciplined seed fund.

## The value chain we invest across

```
Inputs → Farm/Production → Processing/Manufacturing → Distribution/Logistics → Retail/Grocery → Restaurant/Foodservice → Consumer
```

Each stage has a productivity problem addressable by one or more of our four vectors.
We are deliberately **whole-chain**, not single-stage — it widens the funnel and lets
us pattern-match productivity solutions across the chain.

## Demand-side tailwinds worth tracking

- **Automation economics crossing over** — as labor cost rises and robot cost falls,
  the payback period on food robotics keeps shortening; track it per sub-sector.
- **Food-safety & traceability regulation** — compliance burden creates pull for
  Decision-Automation and CV/Sensing tools.
- **Consumption shifts** (e.g. GLP-1 drugs reshaping what and how much people buy) —
  a demand-signal Redstick actively monitors via ecosystem research and briefs.
- **Reshoring / supply-chain resilience** — pressure to localize and de-risk food
  supply chains favors productivity tech that makes domestic production viable.

## Competitive map (where Redstick sits)

| Investor type | Overlap with Redstick | How Redstick differentiates |
|---|---|---|
| Generalist pre-seed/seed funds | High deal overlap | Food operator credibility + sector diligence edge |
| Large agrifood-tech funds | Later stage, bigger checks | We enter earlier, are hands-on, and are not check-minimum-bound |
| Corporate/strategic agrifood VCs | Strategic, slower | Founder-first, fast, no strategic strings |
| Angel networks / policy capital (FCC, CFIN) | Co-investors, not competitors | We partner and co-invest; see section 2 & 6 |

Redstick's position: **the earliest disciplined institutional check with real food-
operator value-add.** That is the wedge against both generalists (who lack the sector
edge) and big agrifood funds (who won't write the first small check hands-on).

## Risks to the thesis (watch list)

- **Robotics hardware capital intensity** — deep-tech burn can outrun a seed fund's
  ability to sustain a company to the next round; diligence weights capital efficiency.
- **Regulatory latency** in bio/materials — long paths to launch; the kill criteria
  screen out >18-month regulatory blockers.
- **Platform dependency** — food-AI companies reliant on a single third-party model/API
  can see unit economics change overnight; flagged as a warning in triage.
- **Consensus catching up** — if capital floods food-productivity, entry prices rise;
  discipline on caps and the fund-returner bar is the defense.
