# SPV & Follow-on Strategy

How the second half of the fund concentrates into winners — and how Redstick supports
its breakouts beyond what a $5M fund can do off its own balance sheet. This is where
power-law construction actually pays off.

## The reserve philosophy

Roughly **half of Fund I (~$2.5M)** is held back for follow-on. Reserves exist to do one
thing: **buy more of the companies that are proving out.** They are not for propping up
strugglers.

> "Follow-on concentrates into proven winners, not to defend losers." A follow-on
> decision is a *new* investment decision, underwritten fresh — not an emotional
> commitment to a name already in the book.

## The follow-on decision framework

When a portfolio company raises its next round, run this before committing a dollar:

1. **Has conviction increased?** New evidence since entry — traction, team, market pull —
   not just "they're still alive." If conviction hasn't risen, don't follow on.
2. **Is it still a fund-returner at the new price?** Re-underwrite to the $5M bar at the
   *new* cap and dilution. A great company at a bad follow-on price can be a poor
   follow-on investment.
3. **Do we have pro-rata rights?** If yes, this is the cleanest way to maintain the
   position (see [`../03-diligence-underwriting/04-deal-terms.md`](../03-diligence-underwriting/04-deal-terms.md)).
4. **Reserves vs SPV?** Can the follow-on fit inside reserves, or does it exceed what the
   fund should put into one name (concentration limits, doc 02)? If it exceeds, use an
   SPV.
5. **Signaling check** — see below.

Decision tree:

```
Next round →
  Conviction up AND still a fund-returner at new price?
    ├─ No  → Do not follow on. Hold. Communicate neutrally. Mark per valuation policy.
    └─ Yes → Have pro-rata?
              ├─ Yes → Follow on from reserves (up to concentration limit)
              └─ No  → Negotiate allocation; if secured and within limits → reserves
                        If allocation > prudent fund concentration → raise an SPV
```

## The small-fund signaling problem

A $5M fund that *doesn't* follow on can send a negative signal to the next lead ("why
aren't your early backers doubling down?"). Manage it deliberately:

- **Set expectations at entry** — founders know Fund I writes small first checks and
  concentrates selectively; non-participation is not a verdict.
- **Be explicit with the founder** when you're not following on and why (usually
  reserve/concentration mechanics, not loss of faith) — so they can frame it to the lead.
- **Use pro-rata + SPV** to participate in the winners precisely so the signal stays
  positive where it matters most.

## SPVs — concentrating beyond reserves

For a breakout where the right move is to invest more than prudent fund concentration
allows, a **Special Purpose Vehicle** lets Redstick (and co-investing LPs/network) put
additional capital into that single company without over-concentrating Fund I itself.

| Use an SPV when | Notes |
|---|---|
| Follow-on allocation exceeds prudent Fund I concentration | Keeps the fund diversified while backing the winner |
| LPs/network want direct exposure to a specific breakout | Deepens LP relationships (see [`../06-lp-relations-fund-ops/`](../06-lp-relations-fund-ops/)) |
| Pro-rata is larger than reserves can cover | SPV carries the excess |

SPV mechanics (entity, terms, fees/carry, allocation fairness) run through fund counsel
and are governed by the conflicts policy — allocation between the fund and an SPV is a
classic conflict and must follow
[`../07-governance-compliance/03-conflicts-policy.md`](../07-governance-compliance/03-conflicts-policy.md).
Treat specific SPV economics as `[confirm with LPA / counsel]`.

## Marking the position

When a company raises an up-round, update the mark per the valuation policy (cost until a

## What good follow-on discipline looks like

- Most reserves flow to a **minority of the portfolio** — the ones earning it.
- Every follow-on has a **fresh memo** and a fund-returner re-test at the new price.
- No reserves are spent to "save face" on a struggling name — that capital is better held
  for a winner or a new vintage bet.
